## Catch changes while they are small

Scope creep begins when requested work moves beyond the agreement without a matching change to fee, timing, or deliverables. It can sound harmless: “Could we add one more page while you are there?” The problem is not the request. The problem is treating it as if the original agreement already covers it.

Compare each request with the signed scope. Check whether it introduces:

- A new deliverable or extra feedback round
- Changed assumptions or another decision-maker
- Content work assigned to the wrong party
- A faster deadline or a moved review date

Record requests in writing even when they arrive during a call.

> A boundary is easier to discuss before the extra work starts. Once you have delivered it for free, the client may reasonably assume similar requests are included.

## Offer a clear change order

A change order states what changed and what that change does to the project. Keep it proportional to the request:

```text
Requested change: Add an Events template and migrate six existing events.

Effect: Adds ₱12,000 and four business days. The revised launch target is 22 March.

Approval: Reply “approved” before 3 p.m. Friday to keep the revised schedule.
```

For a larger change, issue a formal amendment with updated deliverables, milestones, fees, and signatures. Do not begin while approval is vague.

## Say no without making it personal

Use the agreement as the shared reference. A useful response has three parts: acknowledge the goal, state the boundary, and offer choices.

“The events section would support the launch campaign. It sits outside the three templates in our agreement. I can quote it as a change, or we can replace the About template and keep the current fee.”

Sometimes the right answer is no. Decline work that you cannot complete safely, legally, or to the required standard. You can also decline a schedule change when it would break another commitment. Give the practical reason and, when appropriate, suggest a later phase or a specialist.

Keep a simple change log with the date, request, decision, fee effect, and schedule effect. Review it at project meetings. When the client can see which changes were accepted, deferred, or exchanged, the conversation stays anchored to decisions rather than memory.
