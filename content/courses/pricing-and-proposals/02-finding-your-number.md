## Calculate a sustainable floor

Your minimum rate starts with the cost of doing business, not a competitor’s public price. Estimate the annual pay you need, business expenses, taxes, equipment, software, insurance, and a reserve for quiet months. Then divide by realistic billable hours.

```text
annual revenue needed
÷ annual billable hours
= hourly floor
```

Do not divide by every working hour. Sales calls, bookkeeping, learning, proposals, and leave take time but cannot all appear on client invoices. Use your own records once you have them; until then, choose a cautious billable estimate and revisit it monthly.

For a fixed project, estimate the work by phase: discovery, production, meetings, feedback, testing, handoff, and administration. Multiply by your floor, then add a risk allowance that matches what remains uncertain. The calculation is an internal check, not a timesheet you must expose in the proposal.

## Check the market without copying it

Collect comparable evidence from conversations with peers, published ranges tied to a location and service, past quotes, and client responses. Compare like with like. A solo freelancer’s landing page, an agency campaign, and an offshore template setup carry different scope and support.

> Your floor answers “Can I afford this work?” Market evidence answers “What alternatives will this buyer compare?” You need both answers.

If buyers accept every proposal instantly and your schedule stays full, price may be one constraint. If every proposal fails, do not assume price is the only cause. Review fit, trust, scope, proof, timing, and who made the decision.

## Raise prices deliberately

Raise your price for new enquiries first. Choose a date and a clear increment, then measure what happens across several proposals rather than reacting to one reply. Existing clients need notice and a specific effective date.

Keep a pricing log with:

- Project type and scope
- Quoted fee and result
- Estimated and actual hours
- Work that exceeded its allowance

After each project, compare the estimate with delivery. Adjust the next quote where meetings, revisions, or content work repeatedly exceed the allowance. A useful number comes from costs, market evidence, and completed work, not confidence alone.
