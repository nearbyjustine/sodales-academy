## Turn the conversation into a decision

Closing begins with an accurate summary. Send a short written note after discovery that names the problem, deliverables, boundaries, timing, and fee. The client should be able to point to a sentence and correct it before signing.

> You need a five-page site that lets customers view the current menu and send catering enquiries. The project includes page design, responsive implementation, and one feedback round. You will supply final menu copy and photography by 8 October. The fixed fee is ₱48,000, paid 50% to book and 50% before launch.

Present the number in the same calm tone as the scope. Do not apologise, add an instant discount, or bury the fee beneath a long defence. Then stop talking and let the client respond.

## Make next steps visible

A complete offer answers the practical questions:

- What exactly will be delivered?
- What must the client provide, and by when?
- How many feedback rounds are included?
- What events trigger invoices and handover?
- How long is the proposal valid?
- What happens after approval?

Give the client one clear action, such as signing the proposal and paying the booking invoice. Avoid several competing links or an open-ended “Let me know.”

## When they need time

“Let me think about it” is a reasonable response. Ask what information would help and when they expect to decide. A neutral reply sounds like this:

```text
Of course. Is there a part of the scope, timing, or fee that you would like me to
clarify? If not, may I check in next Tuesday after you have reviewed it with Sam?
```

If they name a concern, address that concern. Do not invent bonuses or reduce the fee before understanding the objection. If the budget is lower, reduce scope with it: fewer pages, one template, or a later phase. Keep the relationship between work and price visible.

Follow up on the agreed date once. If there is no answer, close the loop politely and release the slot. A clean “not now” lets you return to finding a better-fit client without resentment.
